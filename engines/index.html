01The big idea

The big idea: An engine turns fuel into motion by burning it in a closed space, again and again.

Every car engine on this page follows the same simple recipe. Learn it once and the rest will make sense.

  1. 01

    Fuel + air

    Fuel needs oxygen to burn, just like a campfire. The engine pulls in air and mixes it with a little fuel.

  2. 02

    Squeeze

    A piston squeezes the mixture into a tiny space. Squeezed gas gets hot and burns much better.

  3. 03

    Spark and push

    A spark lights the mixture. The hot gas expands fast and pushes the piston down hard.

  4. 04

    Spin

    The crankshaft turns that push into spinning, like your legs turning bicycle pedals. The spin drives the wheels.

Why it matters

Burning fuel releases a lot of energy from a small amount of liquid. That is why engines can move heavy cars for hundreds of kilometres on one tank.

Safety

Fuel and engines are dangerous: they get very hot, the parts move with great force and fuel catches fire easily. Please explore them here, not at home.

Go deeper

An internal combustion engine is a heat engine: it turns heat into mechanical work. The fuel burns inside the engine, so the hot gas itself does the pushing.

No heat engine can turn all of its heat into work. Physics sets a ceiling, and real engines lose more heat to cooling, exhaust and friction. You will see where it all goes in the efficiency section.

  1. Suck

    1. Intake

    The piston slides down and the intake valve is open. Air and a fine mist of fuel rush in — like air filling a syringe when you pull the plunger.

  2. Squeeze

    2. Compression

    Both valves close. The piston moves up and squeezes the mixture into a space about ten times smaller. Squeezed gas gets hot, like a bicycle pump that warms up when you pump fast.

  3. Bang

    3. Power

    Just before the top, the spark plug fires. The mixture burns in a split second. The hot gas presses on the piston and shoves it down. Only this stroke makes power.

  4. Blow

    4. Exhaust

    The exhaust valve opens. The piston rises and pushes the burned gas out. Then the intake valve opens and it all starts again.

The engine’s heartbeat chart

This chart shows the pressure inside the cylinder (up) against the space the gas has (sideways). The dot follows the 3D engine. The shaded loop is the work done in one cycle.

Why it matters

Doing the four jobs in separate strokes keeps fresh charge and burned gas apart. That makes the engine clean and efficient for its size.

Go deeper

Crank angle. One full cycle takes 720° of crank rotation: intake 0–180°, compression 180–360°, power 360–540°, exhaust 540–720°. The model computes the piston position from the crank geometry: x(θ) = r·cos θ + √(l² − r²·sin² θ), with r = half the stroke and l = the rod length.

Valve timing typical. Valves do not open exactly at the dead centres. The intake opens about 10° before top dead centre and closes about 45° after bottom dead centre, so the moving air keeps filling the cylinder. The exhaust opens about 45° before bottom dead centre. Near the top, both are briefly open: this is overlap.

Spark timing. The flame needs time to spread, so the spark comes 10–30° before the top. The faster the engine turns, the earlier the spark.

Pressure model. Compression and expansion follow p·Vⁿ = constant with n ≈ 1.3. Burning is modelled with a Wiebe function, x_b(θ) = 1 − exp(−a·((θ − θ₀)/Δθ)^(m+1)), with a ≈ 5 and m ≈ 2. Peak pressure here is about 50–65 bar at full throttle.

Compression ratio typical. Petrol engines use about 9–14:1. Higher would be more efficient, but the mixture would start to knock.

Squeeze much harder

A diesel squeezes air about 15 to 20 times typical. The air gets hot enough — several hundred degrees Celsius — to light diesel fuel by itself.

Fuel goes in last

An injector sprays fuel at very high pressure, just before the top. The fuel lights within a few thousandths of a second.

Glow plug for cold starts

On a cold morning the air may not get hot enough. A glow plug warms the chamber to help the engine start.

Strong but heavy

High pressure needs a thick, strong engine. Diesels are heavier and noisier, but they pull hard at low speed — they make a lot of torque.

Why diesels used to be dirty

Burning fuel droplets can leave tiny soot particles, and very hot burning makes nitrogen oxides (NOx). Modern diesels catch soot in a particulate filter and remove NOx with SCR: a liquid called AdBlue is sprayed into the exhaust and turns NOx into nitrogen and water.

Why it matters

Higher squeeze means more of the fuel’s energy becomes motion. Diesels are the most efficient engines on the road.

Go deeper

Compression ratio typical: about 14–22:1 for diesels against 9–14:1 for petrol engines. Petrol cannot go that high because it would self-ignite too early and knock. Diesel is meant to self-ignite.

Combustion. After injection starts there is a short ignition delay. The fuel that mixed during the delay burns all at once (the sharp first peak), then the rest burns as fast as it can mix with air. The model uses a double Wiebe function for this.

Load control. A petrol engine controls power by throttling the air. A diesel lets the air in freely and changes the amount of fuel. That avoids pumping losses at part load, one reason for its high efficiency.

Efficiency typical: a modern car diesel reaches about 40–45 % at its best point, against roughly 35–41 % for a modern petrol engine.

Up: squeeze and suck

Above the piston, the mixture is squeezed. Below it, the rising piston pulls fresh mixture into the crankcase.

Down: bang and swap

The spark pushes the piston down. It first uncovers the exhaust port, so burned gas escapes. Then the transfer ports open and fresh mixture flows in.

Simple and light

Few moving parts and a power stroke every turn. That is why two-strokes suit tools you carry.

Loud and less clean

Some fresh fuel escapes straight out of the exhaust. Oil is mixed into the fuel to lubricate the engine, and it burns too, so the exhaust is smoky.

Why it matters

Twice as many power strokes from the same size makes small two-strokes light and punchy — great for chainsaws, mopeds and outboard motors.

Giants work differently

The biggest engines in the world are also two-strokes: the slow diesels that drive container ships. They are very different inside. Air is blown in through ports at the bottom and leaves through a valve at the top (uniflow scavenging). Long crossheads keep side forces off the pistons. Their efficiency is above 50 % typical.

Go deeper

Port timing typical in this model: exhaust opens about 105° after top dead centre, transfer ports about 120°. Both close again on the way up, symmetrically around bottom dead centre.

Why exhaust first? The cylinder pressure must drop below crankcase pressure before the transfer ports open. Otherwise burned gas would rush backwards into the crankcase.

Scavenging means pushing burned gas out with fresh charge. In small engines the crankcase acts as a pump. Some fresh charge short-circuits straight to the exhaust: the main reason for high fuel use and hydrocarbon emissions.

06Wankel rotary engine

Wankel rotary engine: A rounded triangle spins inside an oval and does all four strokes without any back-and-forth parts.

Felix Wankel’s engine first ran in 1957. Its most famous home is the Mazda RX-7 and RX-8 sports cars.

Try it

Watch the counters: the output shaft turns three times for each turn of the rotor.

Three chambers at once

While one face sucks in, another squeezes and the third burns. You get one power pulse for every turn of the output shaft.

Spinning, not shaking

There are no pistons stopping and starting. The rotor just goes round, so the engine runs very smoothly and revs high.

The weak points

The tips of the rotor need seals that wear. The long, thin chamber burns fuel less completely, so rotaries use more fuel and oil.

Why it matters

Smooth spinning and very few parts make a rotary engine small and light for its power.

Go deeper

Geometry. The housing is an epitrochoid: x = e·cos 3α + R·cos α, y = e·sin 3α + R·sin α, where e is the eccentricity and R the rotor radius. The rotor’s flanks are the inner envelope of that curve, so its three tips always touch the housing.

3:1. The rotor centre orbits on an eccentric of the output shaft. Gears inside fix the ratio: the shaft turns three times per rotor turn, and each face fires once per rotor turn.

Displacement of a rotary is usually given per face, which makes comparisons with piston engines tricky.

07Cylinder layouts

Cylinder layouts: Bigger engines use several cylinders, and how you line them up changes how smooth they feel.

Car engines often have 4, 6 or 8 cylinders. Their pistons take turns firing, so the push on the crankshaft is smoother.

Try it

Inline-4

Four cylinders in a row. Compact and cheap — the most common car engine. It has a vibration at twice the engine speed.

2nd-order force

Why it matters

Smoothness, size and cost pull in different directions. Car makers choose the layout that fits the car.

Go deeper

Firing orders common example: inline-4 1-3-4-2; inline-6 1-5-3-6-2-4; cross-plane V8 1-8-4-3-6-5-7-2; flat-plane V8 1-5-3-7-4-8-2-6; flat-4 boxer 1-3-2-4. A four-stroke with n cylinders fires every 720°/n of crank rotation.

Balance. A piston speeds up and slows down twice per turn. The main shaking force (1st order) can be cancelled by layout or counterweights. A smaller 2nd-order force comes from the rod’s angle and repeats twice per turn. Inline-4: 2nd-order forces add up. Inline-6: all cancel. Cross-plane V8: no 2nd-order forces. Flat-plane V8: a sideways 2nd-order force. Boxer-4: forces cancel, leaving a small rocking couple.

The meter in the model sums these forces in the front view using cos φ and λ·cos 2φ per cylinder, with λ = r/l ≈ 0.29.

08Engine systems

Engine systems: The engine needs helpers: fuel, a spark, oil, cooling and clean-up.

Pick a system to see how it works. The parts it uses light up on the engine.

Try it

Tap each system, then rotate the engine to find the glowing parts.

Fuel delivery

A pump in the tank sends petrol forward under pressure. The fuel injector opens for a few thousandths of a second and sprays a fine mist into the air. A computer decides exactly how long.

Ignition

The battery gives 12 volts. The ignition coil boosts that to tens of thousands of volts, enough to jump the gap in the spark plug and light the mixture.

Lubrication

An oil pump pulls oil from the oil pan and pushes it to every bearing. A thin film of oil keeps metal from touching metal, a bit like a water slide.

Cooling

A water pump moves coolant through the water jacket around the cylinder. The hot coolant goes to the radiator to cool. A thermostat keeps the engine at the right temperature.

Exhaust clean-up

Hot exhaust passes through the catalytic converter. Inside, precious metals help turn harmful gases into carbon dioxide, water and nitrogen.

Why it matters

If any helper fails, the engine stops — or worse, it damages itself.

Go deeper

Fuel pressure typical: port injection works at about 3–5 bar; direct petrol injection at 100–350 bar; diesel common-rail at 1,600–2,500 bar.

Oil film. In a running bearing, oil is dragged into the narrowing gap and builds up pressure that lifts the shaft off the metal. This is called hydrodynamic lubrication.

Coolant runs at about 90 °C typical. The thermostat stays shut when the engine is cold, so it warms up quickly, then opens to send coolant to the radiator.

Three-way catalyst. It converts carbon monoxide, unburned fuel and nitrogen oxides at once, but only if the air–fuel ratio is kept very close to chemically correct. An oxygen sensor in the exhaust lets the engine computer hold it there.

09Turbochargers and superchargers

Turbochargers and superchargers: Squeeze more air in and you can burn more fuel — that means more power from the same engine.

A turbocharger uses the exhaust to spin a fan. A supercharger is driven by the engine itself.

Turbo lag

Press the pedal and the turbo first has to speed up. For a moment the engine feels like it has no boost. That delay is turbo lag.

Try it

Move the boost slider and watch the torque curve rise. Then press “Accelerate” to see turbo lag.

Turbocharger

Exhaust gas spins a turbine wheel. A compressor wheel on the same shaft pushes extra air into the engine. It uses energy that would otherwise be wasted.

Supercharger

A belt from the crankshaft drives the compressor. Boost is instant, but it takes some of the engine’s own power.

Intercooler

Squeezing air heats it, and hot air is thin. An intercooler cools the air so more of it fits in the cylinder.

Wastegate

Too much boost would hurt the engine. A wastegate lets some exhaust bypass the turbine to hold the boost steady.

Why it matters

Boost lets a small engine do the work of a big one, so cars can be lighter and use less fuel when cruising.

Go deeper

Boost is extra pressure above the atmosphere, usually 0.5–1.5 bar typical in road cars. At 1 bar boost the air is about twice as dense — less after heating, which is why intercoolers matter.

Torque scales with air. With fuel matched to air, the torque roughly follows the mass of air trapped each cycle. The curve here scales the natural torque with charge density after intercooling.

Knock limit. Boosted petrol engines lower the compression ratio or retard the spark to avoid knock.

10From engine to wheels

From engine to wheels: An engine only works well within a narrow range of speeds, so a gearbox matches it to the wheels.

A car crawls at walking pace and cruises on the motorway. The engine cannot spin from zero to 50,000 rpm, so gears do the translating.

Try it

Pick 1st gear and press “Accelerate”. Change up near the amber zone and watch the engine speed drop.

Why gears?

A low gear is like a small front sprocket on a bike: easy to push, but you go slowly. High gears are the opposite.

The clutch

The clutch connects and disconnects the engine from the gearbox. It lets the engine keep running while the car stands still, and lets you change gear smoothly.

Torque multiplication

Gears trade speed for force. In 1st gear the wheels get several times more torque than the engine makes.

Why it matters

Low gears give a strong push for starting and climbing. High gears let the engine turn slowly and save fuel at speed.

Go deeper

Road speed v = (engine rpm / (gear ratio × final drive)) × wheel circumference × 60 / 1000 km/h.

Ratios typical in this demo: 1st 3.6, 2nd 2.1, 3rd 1.4, 4th 1.0, 5th 0.8, 6th 0.65 with a 3.9 final drive and 2.0 m wheel circumference.

Automatic gearboxes and CVTs do the same job, choosing the ratio for you.

11Efficiency and hybrids

Efficiency and hybrids: Most of the fuel’s energy leaves as heat; good engines turn about a third into motion, the best diesels even more.

Follow the energy from the tank. The width of each band shows its share at a good operating point typical.

Typical shares at a good operating point. In everyday driving the useful share is lower.

Try it

Switch between petrol and diesel and compare how wide the useful-work band is.

Atkinson and Miller cycles

Keep the intake valve open a bit longer, and some mixture is pushed back out. The squeeze becomes shorter than the push, so more energy is captured — at the cost of some power.

Hybrids

A hybrid adds an electric motor and a battery. The motor helps when pulling away, and braking charges the battery instead of making heat.

Why it matters

Every bit of saved heat means less fuel burned and less carbon dioxide made.

Go deeper

Ideal limits. Otto cycle: η = 1 − 1/r^(γ−1). With r = 10 and γ = 1.4 that is 60 %. Real engines reach about 35–41 % typical for petrol because of heat loss, friction, pumping and incomplete burning.

Atkinson/Miller. A late-closing intake valve gives an effective compression ratio smaller than the expansion ratio. The ideal Atkinson cycle beats the Otto cycle at the same compression ratio.

Largest engines. Big two-stroke marine diesels exceed 50 % typical, helped by slow speed, huge size and turbocharging.

How a hybrid shares the work

Pulling away: the motor and the engine push together.

12History

History: In about forty years, engines went from a curiosity to the heart of the motor car.

Scroll sideways through the milestones.

  1. c. 1860

    Lenoir’s gas engine

    Étienne Lenoir builds one of the first practical internal combustion engines. It burns coal gas without compressing it first, so it is weak and thirsty.

  2. 1876

    Otto’s four-stroke

    Nikolaus Otto’s company builds a four-stroke engine that compresses the charge before lighting it. It works far better, and the cycle still carries his name.

  3. 1886

    Benz Patent-Motorwagen

    Carl Benz patents a three-wheeled car powered by a single-cylinder four-stroke petrol engine — often called the first true automobile.

  4. 1892 / 1897

    Diesel’s engine

    Rudolf Diesel patents an engine that lights fuel with the heat of compressed air (1892). His first working engine runs in 1897.

  5. 1957

    Wankel’s rotary

    Felix Wankel’s first running rotary engine replaces pistons with a spinning rotor.

13The future

The future: Combustion engines are still everywhere, but electric drives are taking a growing share — what happens next is still being decided.

Here are the main directions people are working on today. None of them is a sure thing.

More efficient engines

Better burning, less friction and smarter control keep raising efficiency. Pairing engines with electric motors in hybrids saves the most.

Synthetic fuels

Fuels can be made from hydrogen and carbon dioxide captured from the air, using renewable electricity. Today they are expensive and made only in small amounts.

Hydrogen engines

An engine can burn hydrogen instead of petrol. It makes no carbon dioxide at the tailpipe, but very hot burning still creates nitrogen oxides that must be cleaned.

Electric competition

Battery-electric cars have no combustion at all. They are growing fast in many countries, while ships, planes and heavy machines will likely rely on combustion for longer.

    17Glossary

    Glossary: Every word on this page, explained in plain language.

    Type to filter. Tap a dotted word anywhere on the page to see its meaning.

    Atkinson cycle
    An engine cycle where the expansion is longer than the compression, to capture more energy.
    Bar (unit)
    A unit of pressure. Air around us is about 1 bar.
    Boost
    Extra air pressure made by a turbocharger or supercharger.
    Bore
    The width (diameter) of the cylinder.
    Bottom dead centre (BDC)
    The lowest point of the piston.
    Camshaft
    A spinning shaft with egg-shaped bumps (cams) that push the valves open.
    Catalytic converter
    A part in the exhaust that turns harmful gases into less harmful ones.
    Clutch
    A coupling that connects or disconnects the engine and gearbox.
    Combustion
    Burning: fuel joins with oxygen and gives off heat.
    Compression
    Squeezing a gas into a smaller space. It raises its pressure and temperature.
    Compression ratio
    How many times smaller the gas space gets when the piston rises. 10:1 means ten times smaller.
    Connecting rod
    The bar that links the piston to the crankshaft.
    Crankcase
    The housing around the crankshaft.
    Crankshaft
    The shaft that turns the pistons’ up-and-down motion into spinning, like bicycle pedals.
    Crosshead
    An extra sliding joint in very large engines that keeps sideways force off the piston.
    Cylinder
    The round tube inside the engine where the piston slides up and down.
    Diesel engine
    An engine where very hot, squeezed air lights the fuel without a spark.
    Displacement
    The total space all the pistons sweep through. A bigger number usually means a stronger engine.
    Efficiency
    The share of the fuel’s energy that becomes useful work.
    Emissions
    The gases and particles an engine releases.
    Engine
    A machine that turns energy into motion.
    Engine balance
    How well the forces from moving parts cancel each other out, so the engine does not shake.
    Epitrochoid
    The curve traced by a point on a circle rolling around another circle. It shapes a Wankel housing.
    Firing order
    The sequence in which the cylinders fire.
    Four-stroke
    An engine cycle with four piston strokes: intake, compression, power, exhaust.
    Fuel injector
    An electric nozzle that sprays a precise amount of fuel.
    Gearbox
    A set of gears that matches engine speed to wheel speed.
    Glow plug
    A small electric heater that helps a cold diesel start.
    Hybrid
    A car that combines a combustion engine with an electric motor and battery.
    Ignition coil
    A part that raises the battery’s voltage high enough to make a spark.
    Intercooler
    A radiator that cools squeezed air before it enters the engine, so more air fits.
    Internal combustion engine
    An engine that burns its fuel inside itself, so the hot gas pushes the parts directly.
    kilowatt (kW)
    A unit of power. 1 kW = 1.341 horsepower.
    Knock
    When part of the mixture explodes on its own before the flame reaches it. It makes a pinging noise and can damage the engine.
    Lubrication
    Keeping a thin film of oil between moving parts so they do not rub and wear.
    NOx
    Nitrogen oxides: polluting gases made when burning is very hot.
    P–V diagram
    A chart of pressure against volume in the cylinder. The loop area equals the work done.
    Particulate filter
    A filter in a diesel’s exhaust that traps soot.
    Piston
    A metal plug that slides in the cylinder. Gas pushes it, or it squeezes gas.
    Ports
    Holes or channels that let gas in or out of the cylinder.
    Power
    How fast work is done. Power = torque × speed. Measured in kilowatts (kW) or horsepower (hp).
    Radiator
    A heat exchanger at the front of the car where air cools the hot coolant.
    rpm
    Revolutions per minute: how many full turns the crankshaft makes in one minute.
    Scavenging
    Pushing burned gas out of the cylinder with fresh air or mixture.
    SCR
    Selective catalytic reduction: a system that removes nitrogen oxides from diesel exhaust using a liquid called AdBlue.
    Spark plug
    A part that makes a small electric spark to light the fuel and air.
    Stroke
    One movement of the piston from top to bottom or back. Also the distance it travels.
    Supercharger
    A compressor driven by the engine itself that pushes extra air in.
    Synthetic fuel
    Fuel made from hydrogen and carbon dioxide using electricity, instead of from crude oil.
    Thermostat
    A valve that keeps the coolant shut in until the engine is warm.
    Throttle
    A flap that controls how much air the engine can take in.
    Timing belt
    A toothed belt that keeps the camshafts turning in step with the crankshaft.
    Top dead centre (TDC)
    The highest point of the piston, where it stops for an instant before coming back down.
    Torque
    Turning force. More torque means a stronger twist on the wheels.
    Turbo lag
    The short delay before a turbo spins up and gives full boost.
    Turbocharger
    A fan driven by exhaust gas that pushes extra air into the engine.
    Two-stroke
    An engine cycle that does the whole job in two strokes, so it fires every turn.
    Uniflow scavenging
    Gas flows one way through the cylinder: in through ports at the bottom, out through a valve at the top.
    Valve
    A little door in the cylinder head that opens to let gas in or out.
